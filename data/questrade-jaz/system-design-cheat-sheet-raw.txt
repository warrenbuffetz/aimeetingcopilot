2. System Design Cheat Sheet
Use for: Design a reliable system, map an end-to-end experience, integrate a payment service, explain how an account feature should work.
Requirements → User/System Flow → Architecture → Failure States → Trade-offs. Think like a PM who understands architecture, not an engineer designing every database table.

Five questions to ask for any banking feature
1.  Who is allowed to perform the action?
2.  What validations must pass?
3.  What changes in the system of record?
4.  What does the customer see at each stage?
5.  How is the action recovered or reconciled if something fails?

Two critical distinctions: (1) The ledger or core financial system may be the authoritative source of postings, while the product-facing transaction feed is derived. (2) An analytics event showing a payment succeeded is not proof of a financial posting or external settlement.

State-design checklist
Entry condition: What causes the state to begin?
Source of truth: Which component determines the state?
Allowed transitions: What state changes are permitted?
Customer representation: What should the customer see?
Financial effect: Are funds reserved, posted, settled or unchanged?
Exit condition: What evidence lets us advance?
Exception handling: Who resolves an invalid or stuck state?
Timeout: What happens if the expected response never arrives?
“Submitted” might mean the provider accepted the instruction, whereas “Completed” might require later confirmation. Neither should be called final recipient settlement unless that is actually established.

G. Common system-design trade-offs
Trade-off
When to favour one side
Speed vs. security
Add stronger controls for high-risk, irreversible actions
Real-time vs. batch
Real-time for customer-visible status; batch where latency tolerance permits
Strong vs. eventual consistency ★
Stronger consistency for critical financial decisions; eventual consistency for derived views
Synchronous vs. asynchronous ★
Synchronous for immediate decisions; asynchronous for long-running workflows
Availability vs. data freshness
Show clearly labelled stale data rather than misrepresenting it as current
Automation vs. manual review
Automate low-risk predictable cases; review ambiguous high-risk exceptions
Build vs. buy
Buy mature undifferentiated capabilities where integration and control fit
Monolith vs. microservices
Prefer simplicity until independent scaling or ownership warrants separation
Rich features vs. reliability
Prioritize correctness for money movement
Frictionless UX vs. fraud prevention
Use risk-based step-up controls
Immediate feedback vs. final confirmation ★
Acknowledge initiation promptly without claiming completion prematurely
Caching vs. accuracy ★
Use cautious cache policies for balances and critical states
Fast delivery vs. technical debt
Accept reversible shortcuts, not financial integrity gaps
Personalization vs. privacy
Use only data and consent appropriate to the purpose
Complex architecture vs. maintainability
Choose the simplest design meeting measured requirements
“For an MVP, I’d prioritize correctness of transaction state, customer clarity, and operational recovery. I’d be willing to defer sophisticated functionality, but not duplicate prevention, reliable posting, or the ability to reconcile failures.”
