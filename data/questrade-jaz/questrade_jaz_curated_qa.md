## Everyday Banking Strategy

<a id="q04"></a>
### Q04. How would you approach making Questrade a customer's primary bank?
*Priority 1 · Navigation: How would you approach making Questrade a customer's primary bank?*
*Keywords: Win primary bank, Primary account, Financial hub strategy, Make Questrade primary, Switch banks*

I think Questrade's existing investment relationships are a real advantage, but I wouldn't assume someone who trusts Questrade with their investments is automatically ready to move their paycheque there.

The first thing I'd want to understand is what's actually preventing customers from making that switch. Is their existing bank good enough? Are they worried about moving their payroll and recurring payments? Or do they simply not see a compelling reason to change?

I'd be particularly interested in customers who already have a natural reason to move money between their everyday finances and investments.

For example, someone regularly contributing to their TFSA might value a simpler way to manage the cash they're setting aside to invest. Someone who frequently withdraws investment proceeds might care more about having predictable access to those funds.

I'd use behavioural data and customer interviews to understand those needs, then identify one or two segments where we have a meaningful opportunity to differentiate.

From there, I'd look at the biggest barrier to adoption. If customers see the value but find switching cumbersome, I'd explore things like guided direct deposit setup or making it easier to move recurring payments.

But if the issue is trust, or that a fundamental banking capability isn't meeting expectations, that's what I'd prioritize first. And that would mean working closely with the Money Movement and Cards teams to make sure we're solving the experience end to end.

I'd also want to test whether there's a practical benefit to managing spending, saving, and investing together. Something that genuinely makes customers' financial lives easier, rather than just giving them another dashboard.

And in terms of success, I wouldn't focus solely on account openings. I'd look at recurring payroll deposits, regular spending and bill payments, and whether customers sustain those behaviours over time.

Ultimately, the goal isn't just to cross-sell banking to existing Questrade customers. It's to give them a compelling enough reason to make Questrade the account they actually rely on.

**Likely follow-ups**

**1. If you could only prioritize one initiative, what would you choose?**

Assuming the fundamentals are reliable, I'd initially look at direct deposit adoption, because that's a meaningful step toward primary banking. But I wouldn't jump straight to building a payroll-switching feature. I'd first understand why customers aren't making the commitment: friction, missing capabilities, trust, or a weak value proposition. Then I'd prioritize the intervention with the strongest evidence.

**2. Which customer segment would you target first?**

I'd start by investigating customers who already move money between an external bank and Questrade investments. They have an established need that Questrade may be well placed to serve. I'd compare segments on unmet need, size, economic potential, and feasibility before selecting a pilot.

**3. What if customers open accounts but don't switch payroll?**

I'd separate customers who see value but get stuck in the switching process from customers who don't see enough reason to change. For the first group, a guided setup or better progress visibility might help. For the second, we need to revisit the proposition or foundational capabilities. I'd validate which problem we have before committing the roadmap.

### Q06. How would you define the Everyday Banking roadmap?
*Priority 1 · Navigation: How would you define the Everyday Banking roadmap?*
*Keywords: Roadmap, Product strategy roadmap, Define priorities*

I'd start by organizing the roadmap around customer outcomes rather than a list of features.

If we want to become someone's primary bank, I think about the journey in stages. First, customers need to be able to fund the account easily and trust that the money is available when they need it. Second, we need them to establish recurring behaviours—payroll, bill payments, and everyday spending. Third, there's an opportunity to deepen the relationship through cash-flow management, saving, and connections to investing.

But I wouldn't automatically build in that sequence without looking at the data. If funding works but customers don't switch payroll, that's one problem. If payroll arrives but people still don't use the card or pay bills, that's a different problem.

I'd work with the Cards and Money Movement PMs to map the dependencies and evaluate initiatives by customer impact, commercial value, risk, technical readiness, and effort.

I wouldn't want three squads shipping individually successful features while the end-to-end experience still feels fragmented.

I'd rather have a roadmap where we can explain how each investment gets us closer to sustained primary-account use, and what we'd stop doing if the evidence changes.

**Likely follow-ups**

**1. How would you decide the first roadmap priority?**

I'd find the largest consequential drop-off in the primary-account journey, validate the cause, and compare potential interventions against impact, confidence, effort, risk, and dependencies. A roadmap should express a hypothesis about the business outcome, not just a set of requests.

**2. What if Money Movement cannot deliver a dependency for six months?**

I'd distinguish a fundamental technical or regulatory constraint from a prioritization problem. Then I'd see whether a narrower customer outcome is possible with existing, approved capabilities. If not, I'd make the impact and options explicit to leadership rather than promise a workaround that compromises reliability.

### Q08. How would you drive direct deposit adoption?
*Priority 1 · Navigation: How would you drive direct deposit adoption?*
*Keywords: Win paycheque, Payroll adoption, Switch direct deposit, Salary switch*

I'd think about payroll switching as both a customer trust decision and a workflow problem.

First I'd identify people who have opened and funded the account but haven't moved recurring income. I'd want to know whether they're holding back because the bank isn't meeting a core need, or because switching their employer and recurring payments feels inconvenient or risky.

For customers who already see the value, I'd explore a guided setup: clear account details, employer instructions, visibility into what they've completed, and reminders tied to the next pay cycle. I'd also consider how customers manage the transition without accidentally missing bills or having deposits go to the wrong account.

But if customers don't believe the account can reliably handle everyday spending or bill payments, simplifying the switch won't be enough. We'd need to address that first with the relevant squads.

I'd test with a defined segment before scaling broad cash incentives. And I'd measure verified recurring deposits over multiple pay cycles, subsequent card and bill-payment activity, retained balances, and support issues—not simply clicks on a 'switch payroll' button.

The goal is to make switching feel worthwhile and manageable, then earn the customer's continued use.

**Likely follow-ups**

**1. Would you pay a switching bonus?**

Potentially, but I'd test incrementality and retention, not just first deposits. I'd compare an incentive with better switching guidance or a smaller offer, and measure the cohort's retained deposits, engagement, and contribution after the incentive ends.

**2. What if the first deposit is delayed?**

I'd make deposit timelines and status as clear as the underlying systems allow, help customers know where to verify payroll setup, and give support a straightforward diagnostic path. I wouldn't claim a payroll payment is complete until the authoritative system confirms it.

### Q09. How would you drive business value and measure Everyday Banking profitability?
*Priority 2 · Navigation: How would you drive business value and measure Everyday Banking profitability?*
*Keywords: Banking P&L, Profitability, Unit economics, Lifetime value, Business acumen*

I think the important distinction is between growing usage and growing a financially valuable customer relationship.

At HBC, I worked on the growth and commercial performance of a Mastercard portfolio with more than 500,000 cardholders. Acquiring a customer was only the beginning. If someone was approved but never activated or used the card, we'd incurred acquisition cost without much ongoing value.

We looked at activation, spending and cohorts, and used experiments to improve performance. We saw active cardholders increase by four percentage points and spending per active customer grow by about five percent.

I'd apply similar thinking to Everyday Banking, although the economics are different. I'd want to understand contribution from deposits, cost of funds, card economics, and any incremental value from deeper saving and investing relationships. Then I'd look at acquisition incentives, servicing, fraud, and operational costs.

A payroll switch might be a great adoption signal, but if we've paid a large bonus and the money immediately leaves, it may not be an attractive relationship.

So I'd look at contribution margin and lifetime value by cohort alongside deposits, engagement, and retention. The objective is customer value that also creates a sustainable business.

**Likely follow-ups**

**1. Have you personally owned a P&L?**

At HBC I was accountable for growth and commercial performance across a 500,000-plus-cardholder portfolio, including acquisition, activation, spending, incentive economics and modeled lifetime value. I didn't manage the issuer's entire lending balance sheet; I owned product and growth levers that influenced portfolio economics.

**2. Would you offer $300 to switch payroll?**

I'd test it if the contribution economics justified it. I'd evaluate incremental switching, how many customers would have moved without the bonus, their ongoing balance and payment behaviour, and the cost of acquiring and servicing them. I wouldn't optimize only for cost per first deposit.

**3. How does banking cross-sell improve LTV?**

There may be value from the banking relationship itself and from stronger retention or engagement across investing, but I'd measure incrementality with comparable cohorts. I wouldn't attribute an existing customer's entire investing value to opening a banking account.

### Q11. What financial wellness feature would you build?
*Priority 2 · Navigation: What financial wellness feature would you build?*
*Keywords: Financial wellness, Personal finance, Cash-flow feature, Product innovation*

One opportunity I'd explore is helping customers understand how much money they can comfortably save or invest after accounting for upcoming commitments.

A bank balance doesn't necessarily tell you what's genuinely available. Say someone gets paid $3,000 but has rent, bills and other expenses before their next paycheque. I'd want to help them see what's already committed and what might be available to set aside.

For Questrade, there's an interesting connection between everyday cash flow and longer-term saving and investing. But I'd start by talking to customers about how they make those decisions today. Do they manually calculate it? Leave too much cash idle because they're uncertain? Or transfer money to savings only to move it back?

I'd test a focused view of upcoming obligations and remaining cash before introducing automation. Accuracy and customer controls matter, especially if external transaction data is delayed or incomplete.

I'd measure whether customers save more consistently without creating cash-flow problems, not just how often they visit the feature.

The goal is to help people make better decisions, not give them another dashboard.

**Likely follow-ups**

**1. What's the MVP?**

I'd start with customers who have predictable pay cycles and recurring expenses. I'd test a simple upcoming-bills and remaining-cash view with transparent assumptions and customer corrections. I'd validate understanding and usefulness before automating transfers.

**2. What if transaction data is stale or incomplete?**

I wouldn't present a forecast as an exact, safe-to-spend amount. I'd show data freshness, let customers confirm obligations, and require stronger controls before automating any transfer. False confidence about available money is worse than a less ambitious feature.

### Q12. What does a single, liquid financial ecosystem mean to you?
*Priority 2 · Navigation: What does a single, liquid financial ecosystem mean to you?*
*Keywords: Single hub, Financial OS, Liquid ecosystem, Unified balance*

To me, it means customers shouldn't have to think about Questrade's internal product boundaries when managing their money.

They might have cash in everyday banking, savings, and investments, but from their perspective that's one financial life. We should help them understand where their money is, what it's available for, and how to move it between products.

But I'd be careful with the word liquid. Deposit cash isn't the same as the proceeds of an investment sale that may still be settling, and registered accounts can have their own restrictions.

I wouldn't want to show a beautiful consolidated balance that implies every dollar is immediately spendable.

I'd distinguish total financial position from available funds, make transfer timing and status clear, and simplify the actions that are actually possible.

Over time, that could support more useful saving and investing decisions. But the foundation is trust: simplify the customer's financial life without hiding constraints that matter.

**Likely follow-ups**

**1. Would you let customers spend directly from investments?**

I'd first distinguish settled cash from invested assets and look at account type. Selling securities introduces settlement, market and sometimes tax or account-rule considerations. I'd explore reducing friction around genuinely available cash, but wouldn't imply the portfolio's market value is immediately spendable.

## Payments & Cards

<a id="q14"></a>
### Q14. How familiar are you with banking, payments and money movement?
*Priority 1 · Navigation: How familiar are you with banking, payments and money movement?*
*Keywords: Banking fluency, Payments technical, AML, EFT Interac, Escrow, Idempotency, Reconciliation, Source of truth, Stale data*
*Status: mixed*

I'd say my strongest experience is where consumer financial products intersect with banking and payment infrastructure. RBC is probably the best example.

We were expanding Avion Rewards to customers who didn't bank with RBC. Existing customers could receive cashback directly into RBC deposit accounts, but that wasn't an option for the new audience. So we needed to pay them without requiring an RBC banking relationship.

We used Interac e-Transfer through RBC's existing enterprise payments infrastructure rather than build a separate payment service.

What made it interesting was that this wasn't just an API call to send money. We had to think through the transaction lifecycle and the controls around it.

There's a difference between cashback earned, cashback available for withdrawal, and money actually paid out. From a systems perspective, that raises questions about the source of truth, how the payout is funded, and reconciliation between what the rewards system records as redeemed and what the payments system actually disburses.

Then there's transaction integrity. Because the systems don't necessarily process synchronously, idempotency matters: if a customer submits twice or a service retries after a timeout, you can't create two payments. You also need to distinguish requested, processing, and completed transactions.

And there are risk-based controls. We worked with Risk, AML and Financial Operations on eligibility, withdrawal thresholds and moving money to customers outside RBC.

My team owned the consumer experience and integration requirements; enterprise payments owned execution. The capability supported around 3,200 withdrawals a quarter, with about 99% processed within 24 hours.

I've also worked on the credit side through HBC's Mastercard partnership with Neo. So I'm comfortable discussing the architecture and the relationships between balances, payment instructions, risk and reconciliation—not just the screen the customer sees.

For Everyday Banking, the important thing is that customers can trust that their money is accounted for and know what's happening when a transaction isn't immediate.

**Likely follow-ups**

**1. Where was the cashback held, and how would funding and reconciliation work?**

A rewards balance records value owed to the customer; it doesn't necessarily mean that amount sits in a separate deposit or escrow account. The payment has to be funded through an appropriate funding arrangement. I'd reconcile rewards redemption records to the actual payout and settlement records using a transaction reference, with a controlled process for failed, expired, or returned payments. Enterprise Payments and Financial Operations owned the exact funding model at RBC.

**2. How would idempotency prevent a duplicate payout?**

I'd look at two separate risks. First, protect the available balance so two concurrent withdrawals cannot spend the same value. That needs an authoritative, atomic balance check or reservation. Second, a logical withdrawal should have a stable identifier or idempotency key, so tapping twice or retrying after a timeout doesn't create another payment. If the request times out, I'd establish whether it was accepted before submitting anything again.

**3. How would you handle stale data versus real-time balances?**

I'd distinguish where eventual consistency is acceptable from where it isn't. A displayed balance may be cached briefly with clear freshness expectations, but authorization can't depend on that display. A withdrawal must validate against the authoritative available balance and protect against concurrent spending. The customer should also see whether a payment is pending, completed, or failed, with reliable status updates and exception handling.

**4. Was the $15 cashback threshold an AML control?**

The $15 was the minimum available cashback needed to withdraw—not a $15 money-laundering cap. The broader controls concern eligibility, verified payout details, appropriate limits, suspicious activity, velocity and exception handling. Risk and AML own those policies; my role was translating their requirements into the product and integrations.

**5. Why Interac e-Transfer rather than EFT?**

We needed to pay customers at other Canadian institutions without making them open an RBC account. Interac was familiar and could work through an email-based receiving flow without the same bank-account-detail collection as a traditional EFT. We also had an existing enterprise capability to reuse. EFT could work in principle, but I'd compare customer friction, timing, return handling, cost, risk and existing capabilities. I wouldn't claim a formal EFT-versus-Interac evaluation occurred unless it did.

**6. What did you actually own versus enterprise payments engineering?**

Enterprise payments owned the underlying execution service and rail. My squad owned the consumer journey and requirements, including eligibility, user-facing states, integration needs and handling of delayed or unsuccessful outcomes. I worked with Risk, AML and Financial Operations so we designed an experience that reflected how the underlying service behaved.
