### IP01 · What's your take on the restaurant industry today?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

My take is that restaurants are dealing with a difficult combination of thin margins, uneven demand and really unforgiving operations. A restaurant can look busy on paper and still have a difficult night if everyone arrives at once, a section is understaffed or the tables aren't turning as expected.

So I wouldn't assume the industry's biggest technology need is simply more reservations. It's helping operators turn demand into a service they can deliver well and profitably.

That makes OpenTable interesting because it sits between the diner looking for a table and the restaurant deciding what it can actually accommodate. My working hypothesis is that the next wave of value comes from better availability, pacing and revenue decisions, with much less manual work at the host stand.

I'd want to validate which part of that problem is most painful by restaurant segment. A fine-dining venue and a busy neighborhood restaurant may have very different constraints.

### IP02 · What are the biggest pain points restaurant operators face?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

I'd put three problems at the top. One is profitability: costs move, demand changes, but restaurants don't have unlimited room to raise prices. The second is uncertainty: a full reservation book doesn't guarantee everyone shows up, and a quiet Tuesday isn't fixed by having a great Saturday.

The third is service capacity. A dining room has physical tables, but its usable capacity also depends on servers, kitchen throughput, party mix and how long guests stay.

Those problems interact. If I push more bookings into a service that's already stretched, I might raise covers and lower the quality of the meal. So before choosing a feature, I'd ask where a specific restaurant is losing money or time, and whether OpenTable can actually change that outcome.

### IP03 · Which of those pain points would you prioritize for OpenTable, and why?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

My first bet would be helping restaurants capture profitable demand from capacity they already have, while making it easier to protect the quality of service.

I like that opportunity because it's close to OpenTable's existing strengths: it has visibility into the reservation book, availability rules, cancellations and, in some cases, operating signals. I'd look for restaurants where demand exists but a late cancellation, rigid party-size rule or poorly paced service means they can't use it.

I'm not suggesting building another basic table automation — OpenTable already has those. I'd test whether more contextual recommendations or better execution of the existing tools produces measurable incremental seated value.

I would put direct labor scheduling or dynamic menu pricing behind that for now. They're important problems, but less obviously owned by this product surface and potentially more disruptive to restaurant workflows. I'd change the ranking if operator research or the data showed a bigger opportunity elsewhere.

### IP04 · What is OpenTable particularly well positioned to solve?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

OpenTable's advantage, to me, is the connection between demand and the restaurant's actual book. It can see the diner trying to find a table, and it gives operators tools to decide when and how that table should be available.

That matters because a recommendation only creates value if somebody can act on it. A general analytics provider might forecast demand, but a restaurant still needs to translate that forecast into pacing, inventory or guest communication.

I'd look at ways of closing that loop: show the opportunity, recommend a specific action, make it easy to approve or override, then measure whether it helped. That's a more defensible position than simply saying OpenTable has a lot of data.

### IP06 · What important restaurant problems cannot be solved simply by generating more bookings?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

Profitability and the guest experience, for a start. More reservations help only if those people show up, spend enough to cover the incremental cost and can be served well.

Imagine a restaurant discounts early dinner heavily and fills the book. Bookings look fantastic, but maybe half those guests would have come later at full price, or the kitchen now has a rush it can't handle. That's not necessarily a good business result.

There are also problems with staff workload, table-status accuracy and the time operators spend managing exceptions. Those might be worth solving even if they don't immediately create more covers.

I'd want the product scorecard to include actual seated outcomes, service reliability and restaurant retention — not just reservation volume.

### IP07 · Where do you see AI genuinely improving restaurant economics?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

I see the strongest opportunity in decisions that happen repeatedly under uncertainty. How much demand will we see in the next two hours? Is this table likely to be free in time? Does opening another seating slot help, or will it overwhelm the floor?

Predictive models could be useful when those decisions depend on lots of interacting signals, but I would start with the action. If the forecast says a Friday service is running behind, can the product recommend a pacing change the host can actually use?

The operator should understand the reason and retain control. I'd test whether the recommendation reduces avoidable empty capacity or late seating without adding guest complaints or staff overload. The model is useful only when the better prediction leads to a better service.

### IP08 · What AI use cases are overhyped or unlikely to create meaningful value?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

I'm cautious about AI features where the novelty is doing the talking. A report-summarizing assistant may save time, but if it doesn't change a decision, the commercial impact could be limited.

I'd also question complex predictive models where a clear rules-based workflow already solves most of the problem. OpenTable's table automations are a good example of existing capability we should benchmark against, not reinvent.

And fully autonomous decisions in the middle of a restaurant service would make me nervous until there's a very strong trust and reliability case. A host knows that a particular table has a stroller beside it or a guest who's been waiting longer than the system realizes.

My test would be straightforward: which decision gets better, by how much, and is that improvement worth the complexity and risk?

### IP09 · How would you balance small independents with larger restaurant groups?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P1 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

I'd start by segmenting on operating needs, not just number of locations. A small independent might have one person running the host stand and making pricing, staffing and marketing decisions. They need something quick and obvious, not a deep analytics suite.

A restaurant group may need central controls, permissions, comparisons across venues and consistent reporting. The underlying reservation and availability engine can be shared, but the workflows around it should differ.

I'd try to keep one platform with configuration rather than custom-build a product for every segment. The decision would depend on where the needs are genuinely different and where the same capability could serve both. I'd measure activation and realized value by segment, not just total usage.

### IP10 · What product investment would you deliberately NOT prioritize, and why?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

I wouldn't lead with fully autonomous dynamic menu pricing.

There's a revenue-management argument for it, but restaurants aren't airlines. Price changes can feel personal, affect the guest relationship and create front-of-house confusion. It also isn't the first place I'd expect OpenTable's existing reservation and table-management data to give us a clear advantage.

I'd rather prove value with more controllable decisions: availability, cancellation recovery and helpful pacing recommendations that operators can approve. Those can build trust without asking restaurants to change how guests perceive their hospitality.

I'd revisit pricing in an environment where variability is already expected, like special experiences or events, if customers and operators actually want it. The question isn't whether we can optimize something — it's whether we should.

### IP11 · How would you distinguish a real industry problem from an attractive technology solution?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

I'd want evidence that the problem is frequent, costly and important enough that operators will change how they work.

Suppose someone proposes an AI assistant that tells managers when to open more availability. Before building it, I'd sit with operators during service. When do they make that call today? What's hard about it? What happens if they get it wrong? And could a simple rule solve the same issue?

Then I'd put a rough dollar or time value against the decision. A small manual pilot might be enough to see whether managers actually use the recommendation.

If they ignore a clear, accurate suggestion because it doesn't fit the realities of the floor, making the model more sophisticated won't rescue the product. That's a workflow problem, not a modeling problem.

### IP12 · How would you determine whether OpenTable creates incremental revenue rather than capturing existing demand?
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

I'd separate revenue attributed to OpenTable from revenue the restaurant wouldn't have earned without the intervention. Those aren't the same.

I'd define the outcome as incremental seated revenue or contribution, where we have enough cost data. Then I'd choose an experiment that can detect displacement — for example, a randomized restaurant-level or market-level rollout, depending on whether diners can substitute between nearby venues.

I'd want POS data if it's available, and information about other channels or walk-ins. Otherwise, a booking may simply have moved from a restaurant's own website to OpenTable.

If we couldn't observe total restaurant outcomes reliably, I'd be transparent about that limitation. We could still measure incremental seated covers under a controlled test, but I wouldn't claim precise incremental profit on the basis of platform attribution alone.

### IP-REF · Industry reference — five pain points & priority ranking
*Category:* Industry POV & Strategic Judgment · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

Top five restaurant problems, in my working priority order:

1. Profitable use of limited service capacity. Even when tables exist, staffing, kitchen throughput, party mix and timing decide what the room can handle. OpenTable relevance: availability, pacing, turn times. Test whether better recommendations convert usable idle capacity into seated value.

2. Demand volatility and missed visits. Late cancellations, no-shows and weak weekday periods can leave otherwise usable capacity empty. OpenTable relevance: demand discovery, guest communications, waitlists. Test net recovered contribution, not no-show percentage in isolation.

3. Thin margins and costs that bookings don't reveal. More covers might require costly promotions or overtime. OpenTable relevance: commercial reporting and POS integrations where available. Distinguish revenue, contribution and attribution.

4. Front-of-house operating load. Hosts need fast, trustworthy decisions during live service; wrong information creates seating conflicts and guest frustration. OpenTable relevance: existing one-tap controls and automation. Explore operator-approved prediction, not duplicating controls.

5. International workflow fragmentation. A translated interface doesn't solve differences in reservation culture, deposits, POS, payments, local integrations and service style. OpenTable relevance: configurable core platform with market-specific extensions.

Ranking is a strategic hypothesis, not verified OpenTable customer research. Validate with operators, product/retention data and market-level evidence. I would initially prioritize capacity-aware, operator-controlled decision support; then cancellation recovery and demand shaping; then broader AI and market-specific extensions.

Existing publicly documented capabilities include Table Automations, Turn Times and Availability Insights, Guest Relationship Management, and conversational reporting in testing. Do not pitch these foundational features as new inventions.

### SC01 · Deposits cut no-shows but booking conversion drops
*Category:* Live Product Scenarios · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* illustrative_scenario

I'd change the deposit rollout rather than declare victory on the no-show rate. We're probably making some diners more reliable while discouraging others from booking at all.

First I'd look at the net number of seated covers and the contribution of those visits, not just the percentage of reservations that show up. Then I'd break it down by first-time versus repeat diner, booking lead time, restaurant type and risk of a no-show.

My likely move would be to keep deposits where the cost of a missed table is genuinely high — perhaps a large party or a scarce experience — and reduce friction elsewhere. I'd test that against the current policy, watching conversion, actual seated revenue and complaints.

If regulation ruled deposits out for certain bookings, I'd remove them for those bookings. I wouldn't try to disguise the same restriction as another payment step. We'd test permitted options like clearer confirmations and waitlist recovery instead.

**Scenario twist:** Legal/compliance says you cannot charge deposits for certain reservation types in a key market starting next quarter.

**Response to twist:** Remove deposits where legally barred; assess compliant reminder and waitlist options. Never disguise the prohibited deposit as another fee.

### SC02 · Faster turns raise covers but hurt experience scores
*Category:* Live Product Scenarios · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* illustrative_scenario

I'd slow or pause the aggressive pacing change for affected restaurants. More covers are only a win if the staff can deliver the service and people want to come back.

I'd check where experience scores fell — was it particular restaurants, service windows or table types? Then I'd sit with hosts and servers. Are guests actually being rushed, are parties waiting even longer, or is the kitchen receiving too many orders at once?

I'd separate different interventions. More accurate turn-time estimates might help, while shortening every table duration might not. I'd test a narrower recommendation with operator control, and use seating delays, complaints, staff overrides and repeat visits as guardrails alongside incremental covers.

If key partners wanted the feature off, I'd give them that control while we diagnosed it. We shouldn't make the floor harder to run to protect a revenue metric.

**Scenario twist:** Top operators say they'll disable the feature unless you prove it doesn't harm brand reputation.

**Response to twist:** Pause for affected venues and return operator control; assess which of faster turns, kitchen flow or guest expectations created the harm.

### SC03 · More reservations, flat profitability
*Category:* Live Product Scenarios · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* illustrative_scenario

I'd stop calling it a success. A 15% booking lift with flat profitability suggests we're shifting demand, attracting lower-value visits or adding costs that offset the revenue.

I'd trace it from booked reservations through actual seated parties, average check, promotions, incremental labor and service mix. For example, an early-dinner campaign might pull guests from a later full-price seating instead of bringing in new visits.

If the problem is concentrated in discounted peak-hour bookings, I'd stop expanding that treatment and test more targeted off-peak demand instead. If there's no path to incremental contribution, I'd end the experiment.

I'd rather report that we learned what *didn't* work than keep investing because the reservations chart looks good.

**Scenario twist:** Data science says uplift is real on covers but average check and mix shifted toward promos.

**Response to twist:** Stop broad scaling if incremental contribution is flat; test only profitable windows and segments.

### SC04 · Accurate AI model, poor operator adoption
*Category:* Live Product Scenarios · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* illustrative_scenario

Eight percent adoption tells me we haven't built a useful enough operator workflow yet. Improving model accuracy wouldn't be my first move.

Then I'd measure *useful decisions*, not recommendation clicks: time saved, seating outcomes, override frequency and guest experience. If people still don't use it, we may have picked the wrong problem, however good the model is.

**Scenario twist:** Operators say suggestions feel like OpenTable optimizing for marketplace fees, not their floor.

**Response to twist:** Treat poor adoption as a workflow and trust issue; do not just buy more model accuracy.

### SC06 · Operator doubts OpenTable incrementality
*Category:* Live Product Scenarios · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* illustrative_scenario

I'd take that objection seriously. A restaurant seeing bookings from OpenTable doesn't prove those customers wouldn't have come anyway.

**Response to twist:** Describe bookings as attributed, not proven incremental, until causal total-business data are available.

### B08 · Where do you see the biggest untapped revenue opportunities in the restaurant reservation lifecycle?
*Category:* Restaurant Product & Marketplace Strategy · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

I'd look at three points in the journey: before booking, between booking and arrival, and during the service itself.

I think the most interesting opportunity is connecting those moments. For example, if an early cancellation occurs, can we tell whether it can be filled without overwhelming the kitchen, then match it to real nearby demand?

Some underlying tools already exist at OpenTable, so I'd investigate where there is still friction or unused capacity. The priority wouldn't be the point with the largest booking count; it would be where an intervention generates genuine incremental value.

### B10 · How would you improve restaurant profitability without compromising the diner experience?
*Category:* Restaurant Product & Marketplace Strategy · *Priority:* P0 · *Evidence:* hypothetical

### B-R01 · Seated covers, bookings and show rate
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

A booking is a confirmed reservation, usually for a party. A cover is one diner actually served; one reservation for four can produce four seated covers. Show rate should be defined consistently: for example, parties arriving / eligible booked parties, or covers seated / booked covers. Never compare the two definitions as if they were the same.

Interview application: Primary: seated covers in the targeted window. Watch no-show rate and walk-ins. Distinguish a reservation from fulfilled demand.

### B-R02 · Booking conversion and demand capture
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

Booking conversion is completed reservations divided by eligible booking attempts or availability views; specify the funnel step. Demand capture is the share of genuine, serviceable intent we convert into bookings and ultimately seated guests. More clicks or bookings are not necessarily new demand.

Interview application: Use search → availability shown → booking initiated → confirmed → seated. Segment by venue, hour and party size.

### B-R03 · Seat and table utilization
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

Seat utilization is occupied seat-time divided by usable seat-time, not necessarily all physically installed seats. Table utilization tracks how much of the available table time is used. Kitchens, staffing, sections and reservation restrictions can make nominally empty tables unusable.

Interview application: Guardrails: seating delays, staff overload, guest complaints. Revenue per available seat-hour can complement utilization.

### B-R04 · Revenue per available seat-hour (RevPASH)
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

RevPASH means revenue per available seat-hour. Roughly: restaurant revenue in the period / (available seats × hours in the period). A 40-seat restaurant open for 4 hours has 160 available seat-hours; at $4,800 in revenue, RevPASH is $30. Define revenue basis and usable capacity consistently.

Interview application: A higher RevPASH is not proof of more profit; check service quality, incremental cost and whether time/seat assumptions changed.

### B-R05 · Average check and contribution margin
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

Average check is restaurant sales divided by covers or transactions; state the denominator. Contribution margin is incremental sales less variable costs to serve them, which may include food, promotions, payment/booking costs and additional labor. It is not the same as gross revenue or accounting net income.

Interview application: Use revenue where cost data are unavailable, but flag profit as an estimate instead of claiming precise contribution.

### B-R06 · Table turn times, party-size mix and reservation pacing
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

Turn time is elapsed time from seating to table becoming reusable, preferably measured separately by party size and service. Party-size mix matters because a six-top holding two guests is different from a four-top serving four. Pacing controls how bookings arrive across time and floor sections.

Interview application: Track on-time seating, kitchen peaks and staff capacity. Faster turns can hurt hospitality; manage actual service, not just the planned book.

### B-R07 · Cancellation recovery and waitlist conversion
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

Cancellation recovery asks how much *recoverable* inventory a cancellation creates and whether a replacement guest actually arrives. Waitlist conversion is the share of eligible waitlist offers that lead to bookings or seated visits. Timing matters: a 6-hour cancellation is easier to recover than a 5-minute one.

Interview application: Measure incremental recovered covers and contribution, not just notifications sent. Watch guest spam and support contacts.

### B-R08 · Repeat dining and guest lifetime value
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

Repeat dining is the share of identifiable guests returning within a defined window, such as 90 days; define whether it means same restaurant or any OpenTable venue. Guest lifetime value is future contribution from a customer relationship, less retention/acquisition costs, estimated with uncertainty.

Interview application: Beware fragmented guest identity and incomplete off-platform visit data. Do not infer full restaurant LTV from platform reservations alone.

### B-R09 · Restaurant product adoption, retention and churn
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

Restaurant adoption should mean sustained operational use: active service days, availability settings managed, or realized outcomes — not just a login. Retention can track active and paying locations by cohort; churn can be logo, location, or revenue churn depending on commercial model.

Interview application: Compare independent versus multi-location cohorts and newly launched markets; reliable first-live-service onboarding is a useful leading indicator.

### B-R10 · Incremental revenue versus revenue cannibalization
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

Attributed revenue passed through OpenTable; incremental revenue is revenue that would not have existed absent a product intervention. Cannibalization occurs when a new booking replaces an otherwise full-price booking, direct booking, other time slot or another venue.

Interview application: Use control groups and total seated outcomes where possible; watch marketplace interference and POS coverage. Attribution alone is not a causal claim.

### B-R11 · Experiment guardrail metrics
*Category:* Restaurant Economics, Workflows & Metrics · *Priority:* P2 · *Evidence:* reference

Guardrails protect people and the system while optimizing an outcome. For restaurant revenue experiments: booking conversion, seating delay, kitchen/service capacity, guest complaints, operator overrides, refund/support costs and partner retention can be critical.

Interview application: Choose guardrails aligned to actual failure modes, define thresholds in advance, and have a rollback mechanism for live-service interventions.
